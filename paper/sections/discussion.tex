\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three principles transfer. Split the accounting state by the question it answers: exact
where the quantity is money and must be settled, one-sided where it has time structure
and must be cheap. Key every charge on the party that bears the loss, and treat the
charge as a transaction with a commit point.

A deployment would replicate the coordinator over consensus nodes, own each subject on
one node so the critical section stays local, and shard by subject range; E22 takes the
first step with separate processes on one host and a loopback network, so we claim a
trusted-coordinator prototype only. A subject's budget would then live on one node, which could under-report a charge;
receipts make that detectable only afterwards, and closing it needs Byzantine agreement
or a proof that the charge matches the mechanism executed. The accounting layer is itself
sensitive---it records which subjects appear in which queries---and accumulators,
oblivious access or an enclave each cost something the design does not budget for.

Two further limitations bound the claims. The monitor is exact over charged subjects, so
its coverage depends only on whether a violation was charged, and E25 measures that loss
(recall 0.014 with an eight-slot table on flat popularity). Of the two adversaries measured, one
re-times its fragments after observing throttling (E18, loss 0.80 to 0.85 of the cap) and
one cycles subjects through the hot table (E14, costing accuracy but never soundness).

Two conditions would falsify the approach. For a small population under a cumulative
policy, exact counting is cheaper (our ablation, $1{,}500$ subjects), and with one
platform a subject-keyed quota is sound while a tenant-keyed one is not. If a query's cost
cannot be predicted, admission must price conservatively, weakening the guarantee to what
auditing provides; the design is for what remains, many subjects over several platforms,
where the promise must hold before the answer leaves the building.
