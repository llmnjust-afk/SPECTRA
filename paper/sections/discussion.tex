\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three principles seem to us more transferable than the specific parameters. Split
the accounting state by the kind of question it answers: exact where the quantity
is money and must be settled, one-sided where it has time structure and must be
cheap. Key every charge on the party that bears the loss rather than the party that
pays, because the gap between the two is the gap an adversary exploits. And treat
the charge as a transaction with a commit point, because a counter that can be
incremented without one will eventually be incremented twice or not at all.

The deployment path we have in mind replicates the coordinator across a small set
of consensus nodes, owns each subject on one node so the critical section stays
local, and shards the ledger by subject range. That removes the single-process
bottleneck but introduces a trust question we have not solved: a subject's budget
now lives on one node, and a malicious node could under-report a charge. Signed
receipts make under-reporting detectable after the fact, which is weaker than the
guarantee inside one process; closing the gap needs Byzantine agreement or a proof
that the charge corresponds to the mechanism executed.

A less obvious problem is that the accounting layer is itself sensitive. The ledger
records which subjects appear in which queries, and the hot table is close to a list
of the subjects about whom the most has been asked, so a reader of the accounting
state learns participation patterns without learning any answer. We have not
addressed this; accumulators, oblivious access, or an enclave for the hot table each
cost something the current design does not budget for, and in our view this is the
most important open problem here.

Two further limitations bound the claims. The SLO monitor samples the hot table, so
its coverage depends on the hot capacity, and sampling the epoch ring directly is a
fix we have not evaluated. And the adversary chooses its split in advance; one that
observes throttling and re-times its fragments could extract more than our ratios
suggest, though the key still binds it.

What would falsify the approach? If a market's population is small and its policy
cumulative, exact counting is cheaper and faster, as our ablation shows at
$1{,}500$ subjects; with one platform, per-tenant quotas are already sound. If a
query's cost cannot be predicted before execution---because its sensitivity depends
on the data---admission must price conservatively or fall back to post-hoc charging,
and the guarantee weakens to the one the auditing literature provides. The design is
for the case that remains: many subjects, several platforms, many tenants, and a
promise that must hold before the answer leaves the building.
