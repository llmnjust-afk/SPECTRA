\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A data trading platform does not sell records. It sells answers: counts,
summaries and trained statistics computed over personal data that many subjects
contributed and that many tenants query concurrently. Blockchain-backed designs
have made the settlement half of this exchange almost routine, with smart
contracts escrowing payment and releasing keys against
delivery~\cite{liu2025yotta}. The privacy half has not kept pace. A platform can
prove that a tenant paid, and that a delivered object matches a committed digest,
but it cannot say whether answering one more query would push some subject past
the privacy budget the platform promised when the data was collected. That question concerns the
accumulated transcript of every query that touched the subject, from every tenant
and every platform.

Differential privacy (DP) supplies the language for exactly this kind of
statement~\cite{fioretto2024differential,desfontaines2019differential}. Its
guarantee is compositional: the loss of a sequence of mechanisms is a function of
the individual losses, so an operator that charges each release against a
per-subject budget bounds the leakage of the
transcript~\cite{mironov2017renyi,bun2016concentrated}. The governance literature
has taken the budget for granted, as a parameter of policy rather than a running
quantity that must be measured, reserved and settled~\cite{kapp2024collection}. Practice has followed suit:
budgets are compiled into policy documents, tallied in offline jobs, and reconciled
at the end of an accounting period, while auditing tools estimate after the fact
whether the released artifacts respected the
promise~\cite{cohen2018linear,ganev2026tight,modi2026revisiting}.

Retrospective accounting is defensible when a single curator releases a fixed set
of statistics. It fails in a market, for three reasons that compound. First, the
accounting unit is wrong. Budgets are keyed by tenant because the tenant pays,
but the loss accrues to the subject, and a tenant that can open accounts at
several platforms---or simply at several tenants of one platform---converts one
budget into many. Second, the decision point is wrong. By the time an audit
reports an overspend the answers have been released and cannot be recalled.
Third, the bookkeeping is not a counter but a transaction. A crash between
admission and settlement either loses the charge, so the next query is admitted
against a budget that was already consumed, or duplicates it, so availability
collapses for no reason. Our measurements make the first failure concrete: a
tenant that splits a budget across 42 accounts achieves an escape ratio of 2.43
against audit-only enforcement and 1.69 against exact per-tenant blocking, and
neither number is visible to the platform until after the fact.

The obvious repair---keep an exact counter for every subject---does not survive
scale. At $10^6$ subjects the counter table alone occupies 265\,MB, it must be
consulted on the critical path of admission, and it must be updated atomically,
which serialises the coordinator. We take a different route, and it rests on one
observation: admission control does not need to know a subject's spend, only
whether it has crossed a threshold. That predicate tolerates error in one
direction and not the other. An over-estimate costs availability, because a safe
query is refused; an under-estimate costs soundness, because a cap is breached.
If the estimator is one-sided---never below the truth---the platform can trade
availability for memory without trading away the guarantee.

\sys\ turns this into a runtime enforcement layer. A conservative Count-Min
sketch over (subject, cost) keys answers threshold queries in constant memory and
is one-sided by construction, because its update rule raises only the cells that
fall below the running minimum. A bounded exact table holds the heavy hitters,
whose budgets are the ones that bind, and a subject returning after eviction is
seeded with the sketch's upper bound for its past so that the exact path never
starts below the sketch. A windowed SLO evaluator and a hysteretic breaker turn a
cumulative bound into a predictable degradation curve, and a two-phase settlement
protocol with a subject-striped coordinator makes admission atomic, idempotent,
and robust to a crash between reserve and commit. The composition algebra is
zero-concentrated DP, chosen not for tightness but because it is closed-form and
invertible: admission must price a query and calibrate a target budget in
microseconds, and the discrete order grid of R\'enyi DP cannot calibrate small
targets at all (Section~\ref{sec:eval-backend}).

This paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We formulate privacy enforcement in a data market as an admission-control
problem with service-level semantics: a per-subject cap over a sliding window, a
tenant model in which the adversary is the splitter, and four objectives
(soundness, availability, stability, atomicity) that must hold together
(Section~\ref{sec:model}).
\item We give a one-sided sketch accountant whose estimator memory is
independent of the subject population, prove that it never underestimates spend,
and show that hot-table seeding preserves one-sidedness across eviction
(Section~\ref{sec:design-accountant}, Proposition~\ref{prop:onesided}).
\item We recast budget accounting as a transaction and give a striped coordinator
with reserve/commit/release, establishing exactly-once settlement and
single-spend safety under concurrent and crashing clients
(Section~\ref{sec:design-settle}, Propositions~\ref{prop:safety}
and~\ref{prop:atomic}).
\item We implement \sys\ as a reference system and evaluate it on thirteen
experiments, twelve of them over three seeds, including an ablation, a
single-seed sensitivity sweep, a settlement-scaling study and a scaling study to
$10^6$ subjects (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

The measurements separate what the design buys from what it costs. At a cap of
$\eps{=}1.0$, \sys\ admits 0.933 of requests with zero hard-cap violations, while
exact per-tenant blocking admits 0.978 and violates the cap 12.7 times per run: a
4.5-point availability gap is the price of soundness. Estimator memory falls from
265\,MB to 4.7\,MB at $10^6$ subjects, and the sketch path costs
32\,$\mu$s per request against 4.6\,$\mu$s for an exact table, a gap invisible next
to query execution. The escape ratio stays at 0.82 however finely the attacker
splits, because splitting does not change the subject key, while audit-only
enforcement rises to 2.43. We also report where the design does not help: striping
pays off only for disjoint subjects, and churn erodes the hot table.
