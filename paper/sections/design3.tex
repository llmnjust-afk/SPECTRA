\subsection{From a Cap to an SLO}
\label{sec:design-slo}

A cumulative cap alone produces the wrong behaviour at both ends. A tenant that
spends a subject's budget in the first hour gets full service for an hour and
nothing thereafter; a tenant that spreads the same spend over thirty days gets
uniform service. Neither is what the subject agreed to, and neither is
predictable for the tenant. \sys\ therefore evaluates a population-level SLO:
over a window of length $W$, the fraction of monitored subjects whose spend
exceeds $\theta_{\mathrm{soft}}=\alpha E$ must not exceed $p$, and no subject may
exceed $E$. The default is $\alpha=0.8$, $p=0.01$, $W=600$\,s and $\ell=300$\,s
epochs, so the ring holds three epochs.

The monitor reads the windowed estimates of the hot table rather than of the
whole population, on the argument that a subject outside the hot set has, by
construction, accumulated less than the eviction threshold and cannot be the
subject whose spend violates the soft threshold. This makes the monitor's cost
independent of $N$ and concentrates attention where violations are possible, but
it also means the reported rate is over the \emph{violation-capable}
subpopulation rather than over all subjects. The tenant-level rate is refreshed
every 64 evaluations, which bounds staleness at the cost of a lag between a
change in behaviour and the breaker's response.

The breaker is a four-level machine (normal, throttle, reject, freeze) that
escalates immediately and de-escalates one level at a time. De-escalation
requires pressure to fall below the current threshold by a margin $h$ and remain
there for $d$ consecutive evaluations; the margin prevents oscillation when
arrivals hover around a threshold, and the dwell prevents a single quiet
evaluation from releasing a subject about to be charged again. In throttle mode
the charge is scaled by $\beta=0.5$, which corresponds to inflating the noise by
$1/\sqrt{\beta}$ and reduces utility; the tenant receives a lower-quality answer
at a lower price rather than a refusal. This converts a hard wall into a
degradation curve, and it is sound because the mechanism actually executed is the
one charged for.

\subsection{Two-Phase Settlement}
\label{sec:design-settle}

Reservation is all-or-nothing over the query's subject set. Under a guard that
covers exactly the stripes of those subjects, the coordinator reads
$\mathrm{remaining}(s)=E_s-(\text{committed}(s)+\text{in-flight}(s))$ for every
$s$ in the support and rejects the transaction if any subject lacks room;
otherwise it records a reservation with a time-to-live. The check and the record
happen under the same guard, so no other transaction can observe the budget
between them. A rejection is therefore not a partial charge: either the query is
reserved against every subject it touches, or it touches no budget at all.

Commit is idempotent in the transaction identifier. A repeated commit for an
already-committed transaction increments a duplicate counter and returns the
existing record rather than charging again, which makes client retries safe.
Release returns the reserved budget and is a no-op for a transaction already
committed or released. Reservations neither committed nor released within their
TTL are expired by a periodic tick, bounding the budget a crashed client can hold
hostage to one TTL; the cost is a window in which budget is unavailable although
no answer was delivered, so the TTL trades availability under crash against
availability under load.

Concurrency is handled by striping. Subjects map to $L=64$ stripes by a hash of
the identifier, and a transaction acquires its subjects' stripes in sorted index
order, which rules out deadlock and lets transactions over disjoint subjects
proceed in parallel. A transaction spanning many subjects serialises against everything sharing its
stripes---correct for a multi-subject query, and the reason our concurrency
experiment shows no throughput win on a two-core host
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-concurrency}). Striping is a scalability argument, not a
measured speedup here.
