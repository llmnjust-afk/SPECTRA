\subsection{From a Cap to an SLO}
\label{sec:design-slo}

A cumulative cap alone produces the wrong behaviour at both ends: a tenant that
spends a subject's budget in the first hour gets full service for an hour and
nothing thereafter, while one that spreads the same spend over thirty days gets
uniform service. Neither is what the subject agreed to. \sys\ therefore evaluates a population-level SLO:
over a window of length $W$, the fraction of monitored subjects whose spend
exceeds $\theta_{\mathrm{soft}}=\alpha E$ must not exceed $p$, and no subject may
exceed $E$. The default is $\alpha=0.8$, $p=0.01$, $W=600$\,s and $\ell=300$\,s
epochs, so the ring holds three epochs.

The monitor reads the windowed estimates of the hot table rather than of the whole
population, on the argument that a subject outside the hot set has, by construction,
accumulated less than the eviction threshold, so the subjects most likely to
violate are the ones the table holds. This keeps the monitor's cost independent of $N$ and
concentrates attention where violations are most likely, but the rate is over the
monitored set, not the population: a subject outside it can still violate when the
$H$ ahead of it do. The
tenant-level rate is refreshed every 64 evaluations, bounding staleness at the cost
of a lag between a change in behaviour and the breaker's response.

The breaker is a four-level machine: pressure in $[0.60,0.85)$, $[0.85,1)$ and
$[1,\infty)$ maps to throttle, reject and freeze; escalation is immediate;
de-escalation drops one level after $d{=}3$ consecutive evaluations below the
current band's threshold minus $h{=}0.05$. The margin prevents oscillation around a
threshold, the dwell prevents one quiet evaluation from releasing a subject about to
be charged again. In throttle
mode the charge is scaled by $\beta=0.5$, which inflates the noise by
$1/\sqrt{\beta}$; the tenant receives a lower-quality answer at a lower price rather
than a refusal. This turns a hard wall into a degradation curve, and it is sound
because the mechanism executed is the one charged for.

\subsection{Two-Phase Settlement}
\label{sec:design-settle}

Reservation is all-or-nothing over the query's subject set, and a rejection is
not a partial charge: either the query is reserved against every subject it
touches, or it touches no budget at all (Algorithm~\ref{alg:settle}).

Commit is idempotent in the transaction identifier. A repeated commit for an
already-committed transaction increments a duplicate counter and returns the
existing record rather than charging again, which makes client retries safe.
Release returns the reserved budget and is a no-op for a transaction already
committed or released. Reservations neither committed nor released within their
TTL are expired by a periodic tick, bounding the budget a crashed client can hold
hostage to one TTL; the cost is a window in which budget is unavailable although
no answer was delivered, so the TTL trades availability under crash against
availability under load.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{Settlement. The log append is the commit point: durable before the state
changes and before any answer leaves. States: \textsc{Reserved}$\to$\textsc{Committed}
(deliver), \textsc{Reserved}$\to$\textsc{Released} (abort or TTL); committed is
terminal and a repeated commit is a no-op.}
\label{alg:settle}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Procedure{Reserve}{$tx, S, c$}
  \State lock stripes of $S$ in sorted order; $st \gets \Call{State}{tx}$
  \If{$st = \textsc{Committed}$} \State unlock; \Return \textsc{Deliver} \EndIf
  \If{$st = \textsc{Reserved}$} \State unlock; \Return \textsc{Execute} \EndIf
  \If{$\exists s\in S:\ g(\mathrm{spend}(s)+\mathrm{inflight}(s)+c_s) > E_s$}
    \State unlock; \Return \textsc{Refuse}
  \EndIf
  \State $\Call{Append}{\textsc{Reserved}, tx, S, c}$; \Call{Fsync}{}
  \State $\mathrm{inflight}(s)\mathrel{+}=c_s$ for $s\in S$; $\Call{SetState}{tx,\textsc{Reserved}}$; unlock
\EndProcedure
\Procedure{Commit}{$tx$}
  \State lock; \If{$\Call{State}{tx} \ne \textsc{Reserved}$} unlock; \Return \EndIf
  \State $\Call{Append}{\textsc{Committed}, tx}$; \Call{Fsync}{}
  \State $\mathrm{spend}(s)\mathrel{+}=c_s$; $\mathrm{inflight}(s)\mathrel{-}=c_s$; $\Call{Observe}{s,c_s}$
  \State unlock; \Return \textsc{Deliver}
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Concurrency is handled by striping. Subjects map to $L=64$ stripes by a hash of
the identifier, and a transaction acquires its stripes in sorted index order,
ruling out deadlock; transactions over disjoint subjects then proceed in parallel. A transaction spanning many subjects serialises against everything sharing its
stripes, which is correct for a multi-subject query and is why striped and global
locking behave alike when all traffic hits one subject. The difference appears
when subjects are disjoint, and Section~\ref{sec:eval-concurrency} measures
both.
