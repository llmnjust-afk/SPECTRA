\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:eval-method}

We run on a two-vCPU container with 4\,GiB of RAM, CPython 3.11, and a single
process. The host is deliberately small, which makes the absolute latencies a conservative
upper bound.
Experiments are repeated on three seeds and we report the mean, except the cap
sweep, which the replication study reruns on twelve; per-seed rows
are committed with the code.

The generator produces $12{,}000$ requests from $40$ tenants over $1{,}500$
subjects and three platforms, with subject popularity drawn from a truncated Zipf
law of exponent $1.15$ over ranks and $15\%$ of requests touching more than one
subject. Each subject carries a cap of $\eps{=}1.0$ at $\delta=10^{-5}$. The
splitting attack adds an adversary controlling $12$ target subjects, divides a
fixed budget of $0.30\eps$ per target into fragments, and issues them under
distinct tenant identities across platforms; we sweep the fragment count from
$12$ to $60$. Identities are drawn by inverse transform from the truncated law
rather than by a power-law draw modulo the population, because the latter maps
large ranks back onto small ones and removes exactly the heavy hitters that make
accounting hard.

We compare against five policies: \emph{static quota}, a fixed per-tenant
allowance that does not measure spend; \emph{token bucket}, a per-tenant rate
limit; \emph{audit only}, which admits everything and flags subjects afterwards and
is the deployed status quo; \emph{exact blocking}, which keeps exact counters keyed
by (tenant, subject) and refuses when that pair is exhausted; and \emph{exact
global}, which keeps exact per-subject counters shared across tenants and platforms
but has no window, no breaker and no degradation. \sys\ is the only policy that is
subject-keyed, cross-platform, time-structured and memory-bounded in its estimator.

\subsection{Is the Estimator Sound and Accurate?}
\label{sec:eval-accuracy}

The first question is whether approximation ever produces a false negative,
because one false negative at admission is a cap violation. Across 24
configurations---four widths, with and without the hot table, three seeds---we
observe \emph{zero} false negatives. The property is not statistical: it follows from
Proposition~\ref{prop:onesided}, and the experiment confirms that the implementation
respects it, including across eviction and re-admission.

Accuracy separates along the population. For the top percentile of subjects by
spend---the ones whose budgets bind---relative error is 4.3\% without a hot table
at width 512, 0.10\% with one, and 0.02\% at width 2048 and above, where the hot
table rather than the width does the work. For the tail, mean absolute error is
0.071$\eps$ at width 512 and 0.013 at width 2048, and p99 absolute error falls from
0.37 to 0.10. Width 32{,}768 is wider than the number of distinct keys here, so its measured
error is exactly zero at 2.1\,MB; it is not the configuration we deploy.

\subsection{What Does It Cost?}
\label{sec:eval-cost}

The estimator costs 27--32\,$\mu$s per request across widths from $2^{10}$ to
$2^{16}$, against 4.6\,$\mu$s for an exact table on the same trace: an 18\% rise
for a sixteen-fold increase in width, as expected from eight memory accesses per
update plus the hot-table lookup. The full admission path is slower and far more
variable (E17): median 74\,$\mu$s, mean 223\,$\mu$s, p99 5.6\,ms over
12{,}000 requests. Profiling attributes 46\% to the sketch and cost algebra, 14\%
to the ledger, 12\% to the controller and 7\% to the accountant. The tail is one
function, a tenant-pressure sweep that scans the hot table every 64 admissions:
disabling it drops the mean to 108\,$\mu$s and the p99 to 0.47\,ms, so the sweep
costs half the throughput and all of the tail.

A single-server queue over these service times gives the deployment picture. At
50\% utilisation the median is 0.17\,ms but the p99 is 107\,ms, because one 5.6\,ms
event stalls everything behind it; at 80\% the median is already 5.2\,ms. A load
averaging 50\% that peaks at five times capacity for half a second makes the median
0.93\,s. The path must be provisioned for its peak, not its mean.

Memory tells the opposite story at small scale. For $5{,}000$ subjects the sketch
occupies 5.57\,MB at width $2^{14}$ while the exact table occupies 1.28\,MB, so the
sketch is 4.3 times \emph{larger}; the crossover is near $2\times10^{4}$ subjects.
Figure~\ref{fig:scale} measures the regime that matters: from $10^{3}$ to $10^{6}$
subjects the exact accountant grows from 1.1\,MB to 264.6\,MB while the sketch stays
at 4.70\,MB (width $2^{12}$) and 6.27\,MB (width $2^{14}$), a factor of 42 to 56 at
$10^{6}$ that widens linearly. Per-request cost grows from 14\,$\mu$s to
27\,$\mu$s, because a larger key space means more hot-table entries and worse
locality; the estimate stays one-sided, with maximum absolute error 0.057$\eps$.

This shows that the structure answering windowed queries has memory independent
of $N$, and that a naive windowed accountant---one cumulative structure per
epoch---would multiply the measured 264.6\,MB by the ring depth. It does not show
that the whole system is $O(1)$ in $N$: the ledger keeps one scalar balance per
subject that has ever transacted, which settlement requires, is far smaller per
subject than a cost vector, and would be sharded in a deployment.
