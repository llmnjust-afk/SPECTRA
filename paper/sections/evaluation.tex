\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Methodology}
\label{sec:eval-method}

We run on a two-vCPU container with 4\,GiB of RAM, CPython 3.11 and a single
process; the host is deliberately small, so absolute latencies are a conservative
upper bound. Experiments use three seeds and report the mean, except the cap sweep,
which the replication study reruns on twelve, and per-seed rows ship with the code.

The generator produces $12{,}000$ requests from $40$ tenants over $1{,}500$
subjects and three platforms, with popularity from a truncated Zipf law of exponent
$1.15$ and $15\%$ of requests touching several subjects; every subject carries a cap
of $\eps{=}1.0$ at $\delta=10^{-5}$. Identities are drawn by inverse transform: a power-law draw modulo the population
folds large ranks back onto small ones and removes the heavy hitters that make
accounting hard.
The splitting attack gives an adversary $12$ target subjects, a budget of
$0.30\eps$ per target and $k$ fragments from $12$ to $60$, issued under distinct
identities across platforms.

We compare against five policies: \emph{static quota}, a fixed per-platform
allowance that meters spend but can neither throttle nor window it; \emph{token
bucket}, a per-subject allowance that refills at $0.05\eps$ per second; \emph{audit only}, which admits everything and
flags subjects afterwards and is the deployed status quo; \emph{exact blocking},
the same per-platform counters with the check applied to composed spend; and
\emph{exact global}, which keeps exact per-subject counters shared across tenants
and platforms but has no window, no breaker and no degradation. \sys\ is the only
policy that is subject-keyed across platforms, time-structured and memory-bounded in
its estimator.

\subsection{Is the Estimator Sound and Accurate?}
\label{sec:eval-accuracy}

Approximation must never produce a false negative: one at admission is a cap
violation. Across 24
configurations---four widths, with and without the hot table, three seeds---we
observe \emph{zero} false negatives. The property is not statistical: it follows from
Proposition~\ref{prop:onesided}, and the experiment confirms that the implementation
respects it, including across eviction and re-admission.

Accuracy separates along the population. For the top percentile of subjects by
spend---the ones whose budgets bind---relative error is 4.3\% without a hot table
at width 512, 0.10\% with one, and 0.02\% at width 2048 and above, where the hot
table rather than the width does the work. For the tail, mean absolute error is
0.071$\eps$ at width 512 and 0.013 at width 2048, and p99 absolute error falls from
0.37 to 0.10. Width 32{,}768 is wider than the number of distinct keys here, so its measured
error is exactly zero at 2.1\,MB; it is not the configuration we deploy.

\subsection{What Does It Cost?}
\label{sec:eval-cost}

The estimator costs 27--32\,$\mu$s per request across widths from $2^{10}$ to
$2^{16}$, against 4.6\,$\mu$s for an exact table on the same trace: an 18\% rise
for a sixteen-fold increase in width, as expected from eight memory accesses per
update plus the hot-table lookup. The full admission path is slower and far more variable
(Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}a): median 74\,$\mu$s, mean 223\,$\mu$s, p99 5.6\,ms.
Profiling attributes 46\% to the sketch and cost algebra, 14\% to the ledger, 12\%
to the controller and 7\% to the accountant, and the tail is a single function, a
tenant-pressure sweep that scans the hot table every 64 admissions. Disabling it
buys back half the mean and almost all of the tail.

Queueing over those service times decides the deployment (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}b):
at 50\% utilisation the median is 0.17\,ms but the p99 is 107\,ms, because one
5.6\,ms event stalls everything behind it, and a load that averages 50\% while
peaking at five times capacity makes the median 0.93\,s. The path must be
provisioned for its peak, not its mean.

Memory tells the opposite story at small scale: at $5{,}000$ subjects the sketch
occupies 5.57\,MB where the exact table needs 1.28\,MB, so the sketch is 4.3 times
\emph{larger} and the crossover is near $2\times10^{4}$ subjects.
Figure~\ref{fig:scale} measures the regime that matters: from $10^{3}$ to $10^{6}$
subjects the exact accountant grows from 1.1\,MB to 264.6\,MB while the sketch stays
at 4.70\,MB (width $2^{12}$) and 6.27\,MB (width $2^{14}$), a factor of 42 to 56 at
$10^{6}$ that widens linearly. Per-request cost grows from 14\,$\mu$s to
27\,$\mu$s, because a larger key space means more hot-table entries and worse
locality; the estimate stays one-sided, with maximum absolute error 0.057$\eps$.

This shows that the structure answering windowed queries has memory independent
of $N$, and that a naive windowed accountant---one cumulative structure per
epoch---would multiply the measured 264.6\,MB by the ring depth. It does not show
that the whole system is $O(1)$ in $N$: the ledger keeps one scalar balance per
subject that has ever transacted, which settlement requires, is far smaller per
subject than a cost vector, and would be sharded in a deployment.
