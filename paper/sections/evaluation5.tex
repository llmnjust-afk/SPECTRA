\subsection{Settlement and Concurrency}
\label{sec:eval-concurrency}

Five settlement invariants are checked directly and all pass: a repeated commit
charges once, a timeout releases, a repeated reserve returns the same reservation and
cannot be re-bound, a crash between reserve and commit leaves no double spend, and an
expired reservation is recovered. Their failure mode is invisible in aggregates: a lost charge only makes the next
query admissible. In
the atomicity test, 16 threads issue 3{,}200 attempts against a subject whose cap
funds exactly 100 charges; every thread count from 2 to 16 admits exactly 100, spends
exactly $1.0\eps$, and records zero violations, so check-and-reserve is atomic in
practice and not only in argument.

Throughput depends on the workload, and our earlier reading of it was too
pessimistic. With every thread contending for one subject, striping buys nothing:
four threads reach 21.4k reserve-and-commit operations per second against 26.5k
for a single global lock, and even one thread is 28\% slower, 83k against 116k,
because acquiring several locks costs more than acquiring one. With disjoint
subjects the same code scales instead: four threads reach 91.5k operations per
second against 25.2k for the global lock, a factor of 3.6, because a transaction
now waits only for the stripes it touches (E13, medians of three repetitions). Striping removes a serialisation point, not contention: a market whose traffic
concentrates on a few subjects will not benefit from it.

Threads cannot carry the coordinator past one interpreter, so we also served it over
loopback TCP to separate clients. One client sustains 1.9k
reserve-and-commit pairs per second at a median round trip of 201\,$\mu$s; four
clients sustain 2.4k at 283\,$\mu$s with a p99 of 16\,ms. The server saturates,
so the limit is the coordinator's interpreter rather than the transport: a
deployment that needs more must shard subjects across coordinators and replicate
them, which we have not built. The bound is a quarter of a millisecond per query, and no more.

Three caveats follow. The coordinator is one process, so the numbers bound the
protocol rather than a deployment, and the cross-process test measures a loopback
round trip, not a wide-area one. A Fabric-backed ledger adds a commit round trip; the
adapter is a stub (Section~\ref{sec:impl}), so we did not measure it.
