\subsection{Does Enforcement Hold?}
\label{sec:eval-enforce}

Table~\ref{tab:enforce} reports the central comparison at six cap levels, each
averaged over twelve seeds. At a cap of $\eps{=}1.0$, \sys\ admits 0.934 of requests
and produces no hard-cap violation, while exact per-tenant blocking admits 0.978 and
lets 12.7 subjects exceed the cap on average, the worst of them reaching $1.69\eps$.
Static quota behaves similarly at 0.956 availability and the same violation count.
The baselines' counters are exact; the violations are caused by the key. A subject
charged against forty tenant budgets can accumulate forty times the cap; on this
trace natural traffic alone pushes a dozen subjects past it. Fig.~\ref{fig:attack} sweeps the fragment count at a fixed adversary budget.

An adversary that sees the decision can re-plan (E18): halving a fragment after each
refusal raises \sys's composed loss from 0.80 to 0.85 of the cap, still inside the
hard cap, but raises static quota from 1.30 to 1.74 and exact blocking from 1.69 to
1.76. A smaller request is cheaper and slips under a quota that refused the full
fragment; a keyed account still charges it. Spreading attempts over forty tenants
and three platforms changes nothing---identity is not the key.

The availability gap is the price of soundness and it is not constant: 13.6 points
at a cap of $0.5\eps$, 10.2 at $0.75\eps$, 4.4 at $1.0\eps$, 3.4 at $1.5\eps$, and
nothing at $2.0\eps$, where \sys\ reaches full availability while the baselines
still violate the cap twelve times per run. The gap shrinks as the cap loosens because the soft threshold at $0.8E$
stops binding: when natural traffic never approaches it, \sys\ behaves like exact
global accounting. The cap of $3.0\eps$ is a control: no policy violates it, since the trace's
natural maximum is $2.01\eps$.

Twelve seeds bound the noise: the 95\% interval of \sys's availability, from
Student's $t$ with eleven degrees of freedom, is at most $\pm0.008$, and the paired
difference is tighter because every policy sees the same traces. At $1.0\eps$ \sys\ admits $4.4\pm0.2$ points fewer
requests than exact blocking and $2.2\pm0.2$ fewer than static quota, while both
baselines exceed the cap $12.7\pm0.3$ times per run against zero. Their accounting
is deterministic, so the only quantity that varies across seeds is the violation
count, and it varies by one subject. Zero violations is an observation over twelve
traces, not a proof; the proof is Proposition~\ref{prop:safety}, and the experiment
is what tells us the implementation realises it.

Figure~\ref{fig:attack} turns to the adversary. Under a tenant that splits a
fixed budget across $k$ identities, the escape ratio of audit-only and token-bucket
policies rises linearly with $k$, from 1.07 at $k{=}12$ to 2.43 at $k{=}60$: neither
has a subject key, so both price each fragment against a fresh allowance. Static
quota saturates at 1.30, the ratio of tenants that can touch a subject; exact
per-tenant blocking rises to 1.69 and flattens for the same reason.
\sys\ is flat at 0.82 across the whole range, and exact global is flat at 0.98,
because neither can be made to forget that the fragments concern the same
subject. The flatness, not the level, is the result: the attack's only lever is
the identity it uses, and identity is not part of the key.

Exact global deserves scrutiny: it is the strongest baseline and it is sound. At
$k{=}42$ it admits 0.929 against 0.923 and leaves 2\% of the cap unused against 18\%. The 18\% is the residue of the soft threshold: \sys\ throttles
from $0.8E$ and throttled queries consume half a charge, so the worst subject ends
below its cap rather than at it. What exact global
lacks is a window, a degradation path and an estimator whose memory does not grow
with $N$. Figure~\ref{fig:attack} therefore shows that subject keying is necessary
and that \sys\ achieves it, not that \sys\ dominates exact global on every axis.
