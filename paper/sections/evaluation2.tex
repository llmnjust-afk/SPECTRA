\subsection{Does Enforcement Hold?}
\label{sec:eval-enforce}

Table~\ref{tab:enforce} reports the central comparison at six cap levels, each
averaged over three seeds. Two rows of the table matter. At a cap of
$\eps{=}1.0$, \sys\ admits 0.933 of requests and produces no hard-cap violation,
while exact per-tenant blocking admits 0.978 and lets 12.7 subjects exceed the
cap, the worst of them reaching $1.69\eps$. Static quota behaves similarly at
0.956 availability and the same violation count. The baselines' counters are exact; the
violations are caused by the key. A subject charged against forty tenant budgets
can accumulate forty times the cap, and on this trace natural traffic is enough
to push a dozen subjects past it without any adversary.

The availability gap is the price of soundness and it is not constant. At a cap
of $0.5\eps$ \sys\ admits 0.754 against 0.889 for exact blocking, a gap of 13.5
points; at $0.75\eps$ the gap is 10.0 points; at $1.0\eps$ it is 4.5 points; and
at $1.5\eps$ and above \sys\ reaches full availability while the baselines
continue to violate. The gap shrinks as the cap loosens because the breaker's
soft threshold, at $0.8E$, stops binding: when the natural traffic never
approaches the soft threshold, \sys\ behaves like exact global accounting. The
cap of $3.0\eps$ is a control: no policy violates it, because the trace's
natural maximum is $2.01\eps$, which confirms that the baseline violations at
tighter caps are a consequence of keying rather than an artefact of the
accounting implementation.

Figure~\ref{fig:attack} turns to the adversary. Under a tenant that splits a
fixed budget across $k$ identities, the escape ratio of audit-only and
token-bucket policies rises linearly with $k$, from 1.07 at $k{=}12$ to 2.43 at
$k{=}60$, because neither policy has a subject key and both therefore price each
fragment against a fresh allowance. Static quota saturates at 1.30, which is the
ratio of the number of tenants that can touch a subject to the cap; exact
per-tenant blocking rises to 1.69 and then flattens for the same reason.
\sys\ is flat at 0.82 across the whole range, and exact global is flat at 0.98,
because neither can be made to forget that the fragments concern the same
subject. The flatness, not the level, is the result: the attack's only lever is
the identity it uses, and identity is not part of the key.

The comparison with exact global deserves scrutiny, because it is the strongest
baseline and it is also sound. At $k{=}42$ it admits 0.929 against 0.923 for \sys,
and it leaves 2\% of the cap unused against 18\%. The 18\% is the residue of the
soft threshold: \sys\ throttles from $0.8E$ and throttled queries consume half a
charge, so the worst subject ends below its cap rather than at it. What exact global
lacks is a window, a degradation path and an estimator whose memory does not grow
with $N$. We therefore read Figure~\ref{fig:attack} as showing that subject keying
is necessary and that \sys\ achieves it without the per-tenant escape ratios, not
that \sys\ dominates exact global on every axis.
