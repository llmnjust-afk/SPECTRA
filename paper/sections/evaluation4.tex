\subsection{Ablation and Stability}
\label{sec:eval-ablation}

Removing one component at a time (three seeds, E7) gives the following. The full
system admits 0.923 of requests at 4.77\,MB and 100.9\,$\mu$s per request; exact
global admits 0.929 at 0.24\,MB and 10.8\,$\mu$s. Removing degradation, so that the
breaker can only admit or refuse, costs 4.2 points of availability (0.881 against
0.923): degradation converts would-be refusals into answers. Shrinking the sketch from $2^{14}$ to $2^{9}$ columns cuts memory from 4.77\,MB to
0.71\,MB without moving availability, and widening it to $2^{16}$ raises memory to
17.4\,MB without moving it either: on 1{,}500 subjects the hot table holds everyone
who matters.

Churn is the cost the capacity sweep hides (E14). Rotating 400 new heavy subjects
per epoch raises the mean over-estimate from 0.16 to 0.28 at a $0.15\eps$ cap and
from 0.25 to 0.65 at $1.0\eps$, and the p99 from 1.7$\times$ to 2.5$\times$ and
2.2$\times$ to 5.1$\times$; at the tight cap it costs 4.5 points of availability.
Safety is untouched---zero violations in 30 runs---because an evicted subject is
re-seeded from the sketch upper bound; but the table stops paying for itself,
bounding what it can buy against an adversary who chooses the traffic.

The hot-table row is a warning. Disabling the table raises availability from
0.923 to 0.930 and lowers cost from 101 to 80\,$\mu$s, but the breaker's flap count
falls to zero: the monitor samples its violation rate from the hot table, so a
capacity of one blinds the SLO machinery. Ablating the table silently ablates the
monitor.



The last row is the honest comparison, and it is less comfortable than it
suggests. At 1{,}500 subjects exact cumulative accounting matches \sys's
availability within a point ($0.898$ against $0.906$ at $1.0\eps$; $0.713$ against
$0.721$ at $0.5\eps$) with 0.38\,MB against 5.0\,MB. Its 19\,$\mu$s per request is
not a like-for-like cost, since it runs no breaker, coordinator or window; the price
of those is measured on the estimator alone in Section~\ref{sec:eval-cost}. A windowed exact accountant is likewise implementable, and
we built one to check (E16, 600\,s window): at $0.5\eps$ it admits every request,
because no subject crosses the window cap, while six of 1{,}500 subjects exceed
their \emph{lifetime} cap. Windowed enforcement bounds a window, not a lifetime.
\sys's case therefore rests neither on windows being inexpressible nor on exact
tabulation being slow at $10^{3}$ subjects, but on $10^{6}$ subjects, where the table
is 265\,MB (Figure~\ref{fig:scale}).

Stability is measured on a load oscillating around the throttle threshold: with
hysteresis and dwell the breaker makes zero transitions, and without them 26, 13 of
which are de-escalations followed by immediate re-escalation.

\subsection{Why zCDP and Not R\'enyi DP}
\label{sec:eval-backend}

The choice of composition algebra is usually a matter of tightness. For admission
control it is a matter of solvability. An R\'enyi accountant evaluates a discrete
grid of orders and converts to $(\eps,\delta)$; the conversion has a floor at
$\log(1/\delta)/(\alpha_{\max}-1)$, which for $\delta=10^{-5}$ and
$\alpha_{\max}=512$ is $0.0225$. A tenant asking for $\eps_q{=}0.02$ is therefore
below the floor, and no noise scale on this grid certifies the target. Our R\'enyi
implementation saturates at its noise cap of $\sigma=1000$, 4.17 times the scale
zCDP computes, and reports a per-charge $\eps$ of 0.023 with
\texttt{reached\_target} false rather than silently accepting the query. Basic
composition, which sums $\eps$, needs $\sigma=240$ for the same target but reports
$0.2008$ after 100 charges, ten times the budget zCDP reports. At
$\eps_q{=}0.1$ the three backends agree to within 0.004\% in $\sigma$ and in the
composed $\eps$ (1.0195 for zCDP, 1.0194 for R\'enyi), and at $\eps_q{=}0.5$ to
within 0.006\%. The conclusion is narrow: for the per-query budgets a market prices,
closed-form zCDP is the only one of the three that both calibrates the mechanism and
composes it without discarding an order of magnitude. R\'enyi DP remains the right
tool for training-time accounting at larger budgets, where its constants win; it is
the wrong tool for admission at $\eps_q{=}0.02$.

\subsection{What Soundness Costs in Utility}
\label{sec:eval-utility}

The utility experiment asks a subject's data to support counting queries over a
fixed predicate until the cap is exhausted, and measures the $F_1$ of the released
count against the true count averaged over the queries admitted for that subject. At $\eps_q{=}1.0$
\sys\ answers one query and reaches 0.823 accuracy, against 0.900 for an unbounded
policy that answers 100 queries and spends 100$\eps$; at $0.5\eps$ it answers two
at 0.552 against 0.881; at $0.25\eps$ it answers four at 0.542 against 0.871. The
unbounded and static-quota policies each exceed the cap on one subject in every
seed, so their accuracy is bought with a violation.

The gap at small $\eps_q$ is the budget, not overhead: $1.0\eps$ at $0.25\eps$ per
query funds four queries, and four noisy answers cannot match a hundred. The loss from degradation
is smaller: 699 of the 5{,}088 admitted queries are throttled, 13.7\% of the
total, and the cost of that degradation in availability is the 4.2 points measured
in the ablation.
